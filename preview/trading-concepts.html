CONCEPT 01
Liquidity
SMC · FOUNDATION
Where the orders are stacked. Price hunts liquidity the way water flows downhill.

What it is

Liquidity is just a pool of resting orders — stops, pending limits, breakout buyers — clustered around a price level. The bigger banks and prop desks need liquidity to fill their size, so price gets drawn to those pools before reversing.

Where it pools

  • Above swing highs — breakout buyers' entries + short-sellers' stops
  • Below swing lows — breakout sellers' entries + long-holders' stops
  • Above/below equal highs & lows — magnet zones traders pile stops into
  • Round numbers ($3,100 gold, 1.10 EUR/USD) — psychological clusters
  • Trendlines — every retail trader puts a stop on the other side

Why it matters

Once you start thinking in terms of where the liquidity is, charts stop looking random. Price doesn't move to make you money — it moves to find the next pool, then reverses.

Pools form above highs & below lows — price chases both
🌊
CONCEPT 02
Liquidity Sweep
SMC · ENTRY TRIGGER
The "stop hunt." Price spikes through a known level, grabs the orders, then snaps back.

What it is

A liquidity sweep is when price briefly trades through a swing high or low — taking out the stops resting there — and then reverses hard in the opposite direction. It's the most reliable signal that an institution just filled their entry by sniping retail's stops.

How to spot it

  • Wick that pokes through a clear prev high or low
  • Closes back inside the range — body doesn't follow the wick
  • Volume spike on the sweep candle vs the previous few
  • Immediate displacement the other way within 1–3 candles

Why it matters

This is where serious traders get their highest-RR entries. The stop sits just beyond the wick (so risk is tiny), the target is the opposite liquidity pool (so reward is huge). A clean sweep is often a 1:3 or 1:5 R:R trade waiting to happen.

Wick takes the stops · body closes back inside · price reverses
⚠️ Common mistake

A break-and-close above the high is not a sweep — it's a breakout. The sweep is defined by closing back inside the range. If the body closes outside, the move is real.

CONCEPT 03
Inducement
SMC · TRAP
The bait. A minor swing put there to make breakout traders enter early — so price can flush them.

What it is

Inducement is a minor swing high or low placed in front of a more important one. Retail traders see the small swing get broken and pile in expecting a breakout. Institutions use that flow to fill the other side of the trade, then push price to the real swing point.

How to spot it

  • A shallow pullback after an impulsive move, leaving a small swing
  • That minor swing gets broken with conviction (looks like a breakout)
  • Price then reverses sharply back through the entry zone
  • The "real" target was the order block / FVG further away the whole time

Why it matters

Inducement explains why "obvious" breakouts fail so often. Before entering a breakout, ask: "Is this the actual breakout, or am I the liquidity for someone else's entry?" If a clean order block sits just beyond, the breakout is usually inducement.

Minor swing baits the breakout · real reversal sits further up

CONCEPT 04
Support & Resistance
CLASSIC TA
The oldest idea in trading — and still the most useful. Price has memory.

What it is

Support is a price floor where buyers historically step in. Resistance is a ceiling where sellers historically dominate. Both come from one simple fact: traders remember levels where pain or profit happened, and act on them again.

How to draw them properly

  • Use zones, not lines. Markets respect areas ±5–15 pips/cents, not exact prices.
  • Higher timeframe wins. A weekly level outranks a 15-minute one — always.
  • The more touches, the weaker. Counter-intuitive but true: every test absorbs orders. The 4th touch breaks more often than holds.
  • Watch the flip. Old resistance becomes new support once broken (and vice versa) — that's where the cleanest re-entries live.

Why it matters

S/R is the scaffolding everyone agrees on. Even traders who don't know about liquidity, order blocks or FVGs are watching these levels — so they become self-fulfilling reaction points.

Ranges respect S/R · zones, not lines

CONCEPT 05
Order Block
SMC · ENTRY ZONE
The last candle before a big move. Institutional footprint, frozen on the chart.

What it is

An order block is the last opposing candle before a strong displacement move. A bullish OB is the last down candle before a rally; a bearish OB is the last up candle before a sell-off. The idea: institutions left unfilled orders there, so when price returns to that zone, they get filled again.

How to spot it

  • Find a strong move that broke market structure
  • Mark the last candle of the opposite colour before that move
  • Draw a box from the candle's open to wick (or open to body)
  • Watch for price to retrace into the box — that's the entry zone

Why it matters

Order blocks give you specific, defined zones to react in — not vague levels. Combined with a liquidity sweep into the OB, you get the highest-probability entries the SMC framework offers.

Last down candle before the rally = bullish OB

CONCEPT 06
Fair Value Gap (FVG)
SMC · IMBALANCE
A 3-candle imbalance where price moved too fast. The market wants to fill it.

What it is

A Fair Value Gap (also called an imbalance) is the price range where buyers and sellers didn't transact properly — usually because of an aggressive 3-candle sequence. On a bullish FVG, the gap is the space between candle 1's high and candle 3's low (where candle 2 didn't overlap either).

How to spot it

  • Look for a candle with a large body (the displacement candle)
  • Check the candle before and after — if their wicks don't overlap, there's a gap
  • Mark the unfilled price range — that's your FVG
  • Price tends to return and "fill the gap" before continuing

Why it matters

FVGs are magnets. The market is efficient and dislikes imbalance — unfilled FVGs from earlier sessions or the prev day are some of the highest-probability return-to-zone targets on the chart.

Bullish FVG · price will usually return to fill

CONCEPT 07
BOS & CHoCH
SMC · STRUCTURE
Two letters that tell you if the trend is intact (BOS) or breaking (CHoCH).

BOS — Break of Structure

A BOS is when price breaks the most recent significant high (in an uptrend) or low (in a downtrend) — confirming the trend is still in control. Higher highs = bullish BOS. Lower lows = bearish BOS.

CHoCH — Change of Character

A CHoCH is the first break in the opposite direction. After an uptrend of higher highs and higher lows, the first lower low is a bearish CHoCH — the first hint the trend may be flipping.

How to use them

  • Trade in the direction of the most recent BOS on your higher timeframe
  • Wait for a CHoCH on the lower timeframe to confirm a reversal entry
  • BOS + retrace into OB or FVG = textbook continuation entry
  • CHoCH + retrace into the OB that caused it = textbook reversal entry
Series of BOS = trend · first break the other way = CHoCH
⚖️
CONCEPT 08
Supply & Demand Zones
FOUNDATION
The big-picture cousin of order blocks — bigger zones, bigger moves.

What they are

Demand zones are price areas where strong buying came in and price exploded up. Supply zones are the opposite — heavy selling that caused a sharp drop. Whenever price returns to these zones, more orders typically rest there.

The patterns

  • Drop-Base-Rally (DBR) → bullish demand zone at the base
  • Rally-Base-Rally (RBR) → continuation demand
  • Rally-Base-Drop (RBD) → bearish supply zone at the base
  • Drop-Base-Drop (DBD) → continuation supply

Why it matters

S/D zones are broader and more forgiving than order blocks — better for swing traders. Combine HTF S/D zones with LTF order blocks and FVGs and you've got the institutional framework most prop desks teach.

Supply & demand zones · price respects them
